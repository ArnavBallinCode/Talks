Motivation

Why does this problem matter?

Organizations talk through email and chat. These logs show who really works with whom, not just the org chart.

The worry: silos

A silo is a group that talks a lot inside itself and very little to the rest of the company. Silos cause duplicated work and slow decisions.

An earlier research preprint (2025) proposed clique-based scores to spot silo-like structure, but only on one static picture.

Our step: measure the scores every month and try to forecast the next month.

Plain-English glossary 1: the network words

Network / graph — a picture of connections: dots (people) and lines (they talked).

Node · edge — a dot · a line between two dots.

Clique — a small team where everyone has talked to everyone else.

Snapshot — the network built from one 30-day window of emails.

Foundations from zero

What does "tension" mean here?

Tension measures how much a clique is entangled with the other cliques in the same month.

For two cliques A and B, ICCS (Inter-Clique Conflict Score) counts the people who sit on the border between them (people in one who talk to the other), divided by their combined size. A fuller explanation, step by step, comes in the next section.

A clique's tension = the sum of its ICCS with all other cliques that month.

SRI = Silo Risk Index: the same idea per person instead of per group (defined later).

Caution (stated in the paper): high ICCS means groups are tightly coupled at their boundary. That is almost the opposite of isolation, so ICCS and SRI are used here only as descriptive measures, not proven silo detectors.

Say

Tension is a number per clique and month: the sum of boundary overlaps with all other cliques. Exact formulas are on the next section. Be upfront that these scores are descriptive; the paper says validity against external labels is untested.

Likely question

"Does tension measure silos?" No evidence yet. SRI correlates 0.82 with how many cliques a person belongs to.

Metric 1, from scratch

ICCS in plain English, before any formula

The question ICCS answers: "How much do these two teams rub against each other?"

  1. Take two teams that each have their own tight circle (two cliques).
  2. Look at their border: who in one team also talks to someone in the other team? Those people are the contact points.
  3. Count the contact points. More contact points = more entangled.
  4. Divide by the size of both teams, so two big teams are not unfairly scored higher than two small ones.

Result: a score from 0 (no contact) up to 1 (very tangled). Name: Inter-Clique Conflict Score = ICCS.

Think of it as: two offices sharing a corridor. The more people walking between them, the higher the score.

Say

Before I show the formula: ICCS counts how many people sit on the border between two teams and divides by the total number of people in both teams. In the picture, people 2, 3 and 4 are the contact points. Next slide gives the exact formula.

Avoid

Do not describe ICCS as measuring isolation; it measures how tightly teams touch.

Metric 1

ICCS: the formula

ICCS(A, B) = contact points between A and Bsize of A + size of B

Contact point = a person in one team who has talked to someone in the other team. The score is between 0 and 1.

Worked example (illustrative)

A = {1,2,3}, B = {4,5,6}, extra lines 3–4 and 2–4.
Contact points: persons 2, 3 and 4 → 3.
ICCS = 3 ÷ (3 + 3) = 0.5

Say

Count the people on the border between the two teams and divide by the total number of people in both. Here that is three over six, so 0.5. High means tangled together; it does not mean isolated.

Metrics 2 & 3

Tension and SRI

From the paper
T(C) = ΣC′ ≠ C ICCS(C, C′)     SRI(v) = ΣC ∋ v T(C)

T(C): sum of the clique's ICCS with every other clique that month. SRI(v): person v's risk = total tension of all cliques v belongs to.

Illustrative

Clique C touches three others with ICCS 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 → T(C) = 1.0.
Person v is in cliques with T = 1.0 and 2.5 → SRI(v) = 3.5.

Two facts reported in the paper

  • Because T sums over all other cliques, it grows with their number: corr. of mean tension and clique count across months = 0.94.
  • SRI behaves like a membership count: Spearman 0.82 with clique count, 0.67 with degree.

→ This is why the paper also uses a normalized tension T̃ = T / max(Kk−1, 1).

Say

Tension adds up ICCS across all partners; SRI pushes the clique number down to people. Two honest facts: tension scales with how many cliques exist, and SRI is largely a count of memberships.

Likely question

"So is SRI meaningful?" Needs external labels (departments, job titles) – listed as future work.

Illustrative: edge thickness = ICCS

Say

The meta-graph is a graph of groups. Each group carries five numbers. The meta-graph is dense: most cliques touch most others, which matters for the GCN later.

Time

Tracking cliques across months

Cliques have no ID. We link month k to k+1 with Jaccard similarity:

From the paper
J(Ci(k), Cj(k+1)) = |Ci ∩ Cj||Ci ∪ Cj|

Matching is one-to-one and greedy: sort pairs with J ≥ τ (τ = 0.5) by J; accept a pair if neither clique is used yet.

Illustrative

{1,2,3,4} vs {1,2,3,5}: shared 3, union 5 → J = 0.6 (same clique).
{1,2,3,4} vs {1,2,7,8}: shared 2, union 6 → J = 0.33 (dissolved).

Harsh for small groups: a 4-person clique that swaps 2 members has J = 2/6 ≈ 0.33 and counts as dissolved.

Result: a partial injective map πk from cliques at k to cliques at k+1.

Say

To say "the same clique next month", we compare member sets with Jaccard: overlap divided by union. Half or more means same. Each new clique can be claimed once.

Likely question

"Why 0.5?" It is a convention; the paper tests τ from 0.3 to 0.7 and survival goes from 56% to 22%.

Survives

The team is found again next month (member overlap J ≥ 0.5).

Dissolves

No clique next month matches it well enough.

Age

How many months in a row we have already seen this team. Older teams tend to survive more often.

In the paper, survival is written s = 1 (survives) or s = 0 (dissolves).

Say

Green lines are matches, dashed red circles dissolve, amber ones are new teams. Survival is just yes or no, and age counts how long a team has lasted. The diagram is illustrative.

Turnover

Why do ~60% of cliques "disappear"?

Of 689 retained cliques: 278 (40.3%) have a successor; 411 (59.7%) do not. Looking at the 411:

Part of it is how we define groups

  • 55.3% of retained cliques are exactly one email's recipient list (a cc list, not a team).
  • The J ≥ 0.5 rule is strict for small groups.
  • 10% are cut by the top-30 limit.

Other group definitions still show survival of only 30–44% (cliques not in one email 30.5%; repeated pairs 43.8%; clique percolation 32.8%; Louvain 36.4%).

Say

Sixty percent dissolution is partly real and partly measurement. A third of the "dissolutions" are partial changes, a sixth are matching conflicts, a tenth are a top-30 artefact. Even with other definitions survival stays between about 30 and 44%.

Likely question

"So is turnover fake?" Partly artefact, but it remains high under every definition tried.

Targets

Protocol A vs Protocol B

Definitions from the paper

Protocol A — deployable

yiA = si(k) · T(k+1)(Cπk(i))

Score every clique; dissolved ones get target 0. What you face in real use.

Protocol B — oracle-conditioned

yiB = T(k+1)(Cπk(i))   for si=1

Score only survivors. Survival is known only after the fact, so it conditions on the future (survivorship bias).

Illustrative example

3 cliques, tensions now: 8, 5, 6. Next month only clique 1 survives (tension 7). A targets: 7, 0, 0. Persistence predicts 8, 5, 6 → MSE = (1+25+36)/3 = 20.7. Predicting 2,2,2 gives 11 – "predict small" wins. B: only clique 1, error 1 – looks great but uses the future. Persistence × 0.4 → 3.2, 2, 2.4 → MSE = 8.1.

Say

A is the honest protocol; B is useful to study the tension part alone but cheats. The toy example shows why both can mislead. The original paper's headline win was a Protocol B number.

Common misunderstanding

Protocol B is not wrong; it just cannot be deployed.

Before the models

Plain-English glossary 2: the forecasting words

Forecast — a prediction of next month's value.

Persistence — the simplest forecast: "next month = this month".

Baseline — a simple method every fancy model must beat to be worth using.

MSE (mean squared error) — average of (prediction − truth)². Lower is better.

MSE ratio — model error ÷ persistence error. Below 1 = better than persistence.

Ridge AR — a straight-line forecast from the value's own past, with a penalty against overfitting.

GBDT (gradient-boosted decision trees) — many small if-then flowcharts added together.

MLP (multi-layer perceptron) — a basic neural network. GCN (graph convolutional network) and GraphSAGE — neural networks that also look at neighbours. GRU (gated recurrent unit) — adds memory of past months.

Overfitting — memorising the training data instead of learning a rule that works on new data.

Say

Second vocabulary check, for the models and measures. The key one is persistence: the dumb forecast that everything is compared to.

Baselines first

Persistence: "next month = this month"

Stated in the paper
ŷi = T(k)(Ci)
  • No parameters, no learning, cannot overfit.
  • Scales automatically with whatever tension level the month has.
  • Tension is sticky: current and next-month tension correlate (r ≈ 0.67–0.72 in the paper).
Illustrative

Tension now = 8 → predict 8.
Truth next month = 7 → error 1, squared error 1.

Why it is such a strong baseline: any learned model must find signal beyond "stay the same", and must not add noise from its own parameters.

Say

Persistence is the bar: copy this month. It has no parameters, so it cannot overfit, and tension is persistent. This is the same lesson as in intermittent-demand forecasting and dynamic-graph benchmarks.

Likely question

"Isn't that a trivial baseline?" That is exactly the point: it is hard to beat.

A straight line learned from data: next = a × now + b.

Neural networks 1

A neuron and the MLP

A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias, then bends it with an activation. ReLU(z) = max(0, z).

Standard
h = ReLU( w1x1 + w2x2 + … + b )
Illustrative

x = (size 4, tension 8), w = (0.5, 0.2), b = −1:
z = 2 + 1.6 − 1 = 2.6 → h = 2.6. With b = −5: z = −1.4 → h = 0.

Layers of neurons; each layer's output feeds the next. Weights and biases are learned.

The paper's MLP encoder uses the same clique features with no neighbours: every clique is processed independently.

Say

A neuron multiplies, adds, and clips negatives. Stack them to get an MLP. Training adjusts weights to reduce error. Crucially, our MLP sees one clique at a time: it ignores the graph. That makes it the "no graph" reference.

Neural networks 2

Graph neural networks: use the neighbours

Idea: a clique's neighbours in the meta-graph may carry information about its future. A graph neural network lets each node collect information from its neighbours.

  1. Each node starts with its feature vector.
  2. It aggregates (averages) neighbours' vectors.
  3. A neural layer transforms the result.
  4. Repeat → information travels 1, then 2 hops.

Meta-graph: each clique can "listen" to the others

Why it could help: tension is defined by the surrounding cliques, so neighbours should matter.

Say

A GNN lets each clique look at its neighbours. Because tension is literally defined through neighbours, it was reasonable to expect help. The next slides show the exact computation and why it can backfire here.

Neural networks 3

Over-smoothing and the dense meta-graph

Over-smoothing: repeated neighbour averaging makes node representations more and more alike.

Why it is a concern here

Our meta-graph is dense: most cliques overlap with most others. Two rounds of averaging can wash out a clique's own tension, the strongest predictor.

What the experiments show

  • Plain GCN (no direct access to tension): ratio 1.15; it cannot even match persistence on its own training data (MSE 14.6 vs 12.9).
  • Residual GCN: ratio 1.00; best validation point at epoch 0.

Honest interpretation

The paper states the blurring explanation, but GraphSAGE, which keeps the clique's own signal, did not improve either. So over-smoothing is a plausible partial explanation, not the whole story, and was not isolated by a dedicated experiment.

Not a claim that GCNs are ineffective in general: these configurations did not beat persistence on this small dataset.

Say

The GCN does worse than persistence when it must rebuild the value from scratch. We blame the smoothing, but we also tested a design that avoids it, and it did not help. So the safest statement: graph information did not provide reliable improvement here.

Likely question

"Did you prove over-smoothing?" No: plausible, partial, untested directly.

Neural networks

GraphSAGE: keep "me" and "my neighbours" separate

The GCN mixes a clique with its neighbours in one average. GraphSAGE keeps two paths:

+

→
new representation
Illustrative

Own tension 9, neighbours 3, 6, 4 (average 4.33). Mixing 80% own + 20% neighbours → 8.07. The clique keeps its identity.

Why we tried it: to avoid the blurring of the GCN.

Result

Ratio 1.00: same as persistence and the same as the MLP. Looking at neighbours did not help here.

Say

GraphSAGE keeps the clique own value separate from the neighbour average, so its own tension is not averaged away. We tried it for exactly that reason. It performed the same as the MLP, so neighbour information added nothing measurable.

Neural networks

Memory over time: the GRU

Idea: a team that has existed for 5 months may behave differently from a new team. A GRU (gated recurrent unit) lets the model remember a team's earlier months.

last month's memory
+
this month's data
→
updated memory

A "gate" decides how much old memory to keep and how much to replace. A team with no earlier month simply starts with no memory.

Why it could not help much

Most teams have only one or two earlier months, so there is little to remember.

Result

GCN + GRU ratio 1.02, the same as the GCN without memory.

This does not mean memory never helps; it did not help here.

Say

The GRU is a memory unit. It carries a team past behaviour into this month. With cliques that mostly last only one or two months there is almost nothing to remember, and the result was the same as without memory. I am not claiming memory is useless in general.

Tension head

Gives next month's tension. It uses softplus, a smooth function that keeps the answer positive.

Survival head

Gives a probability. It uses a sigmoid, which squeezes any number into 0–1.

Why two heads? They answer two different questions: Will this team still exist? and How big will its tension be if it does?

Say

All the neural models share this layout. The encoder builds a summary of each clique; one head predicts the tension, the other predicts the survival probability.

Neural networks 6

Plain vs residual tension head

Plain

T̂ = Softplus( gc(z) )

Must build the whole number from scratch. At the start gc ≈ 0 → T̂ ≈ ln 2 ≈ 0.69, far from typical tensions (~6.5).

Residual (used for the main models)

T̂ = Softplus( T(k) + gc(z) )

The last layer of gc starts at zero. With T = 8: T̂ = ln(1+e8) ≈ 8.0: the model starts at persistence.

Why this matters for a fair comparison: a residual model begins exactly at the baseline and moves away only if that lowers the validation loss. A plain model must first rediscover persistence. Any neural advantage must therefore be real signal.

What happened: residual GCN and SAGE stayed at persistence (best validation at epoch 0); plain GCN was 15% worse than persistence (ratio 1.15).

Say

This design came from review feedback: it removes the unfair advantage persistence had. Residual models start identical to persistence, so if they cannot improve, it is evidence there is little to gain.

Likely question

"Is softplus(8) exactly 8?" ln(1+e^8)=8.0003; practically yes.

Training options

Three ways to train

Zero-fill

Train on all cliques. Dissolved ones count as tension 0.

Conditional-only

Train on survivors only. No survival prediction.

Two-stage (hurdle)

Train both heads: survival chance and tension.

Why compare them? Conditional-only vs two-stage shows whether the survival head helps the tension forecast. Zero-fill vs conditional-only shows whether training on the right rows matters.

Result: two-stage and conditional-only are about the same (ratio 0.99 for the MLP, 1.00 for the GCN). The survival head does not improve the tension forecast for survivors.

Say

Three training styles. The two-stage version comes from statistics for data with many zeros: first model whether it is zero, then how big it is. In our results it makes no difference to the tension forecast.

Survival modelling

The two-stage forecast

forecast = chance of surviving × tension if it survives
Illustrative example

Survival chance 70%, tension if it survives 8:
forecast = 0.7 × 8 + 0.3 × 0 = 5.6

With squared error, the best single guess is this average outcome.

Same idea, no neural network

Persistence × survival chance. We tried five ways to get the chance, from "the overall survival rate" up to a hazard model: a simple model where the survival chance depends on how old the team is (plus its size and tension).

Say

If a clique has a seventy percent chance to live and would have tension eight, the best single forecast is five point six. The same trick works with plain persistence: multiply it by a survival chance. The hazard model is just a simple model of survival by age.

Evaluation methodology

Why evaluation design matters: the collapse trap

On the chronological test window (bankruptcy months, 35 survivors), surviving cliques had much lower tension than in earlier years (mean 4.23 vs 6.17). Any model that forecasts low looks good there.

Collapse window MSE on survivors (five seeds): persistence 5.04; plain GCN (two-stage) 2.62 ± 0.67; residual models 4.91–5.29.

Same model, two verdicts

Plain GCN looks 48% better than persistence on the collapse window, but over 24 rolling origins it is 15% worse (ratio 1.15).

Rolling origin mimics real deployment (always forecast the next unseen month), covers 20 pre-collapse origins, and avoids one special period.

Say

This is a key message of the paper: a short special window can reverse the conclusion. On the bankruptcy months a plain GCN appears dramatically better; across the whole history it is worse. That is why we use rolling origin.

Avoid

2.62 vs 5.04 is 48% lower MSE; do not call it significant.

Statistics 1

MSE and the MSE ratio

Standard definition
MSE = 1n Σi=1n (ŷi − yi)²
ratio = MSEmodelMSEpersistence

ŷ = forecast, y = truth, n = number of cliques pooled over the 24 origins. Ratio < 1 = better than persistence; = 1 = same; > 1 = worse.

Illustrative

Persistence MSE 10, model MSE 9.6 → ratio 0.96 (4% better). Model MSE 11.5 → 1.15 (15% worse).

Key lesson: a lower point estimate does not establish significance. The next slides ask: is a 4% difference bigger than what chance would produce?

The paper also reports MAE and median absolute error. For Protocol A the reference is persistence × base rate.

Say

Our main number is the ratio of mean squared errors. Below one is better than persistence. But a ratio of 0.96 on 237 survivors in 24 months could be noise, hence the tests.

Checking results

Is a difference real, or just luck?

1 · Confidence range

A ratio of 0.96 looks better, but how much could it change with different months? We re-draw the months many times (in blocks of 3 consecutive months, since neighbouring months are similar) to get a range. If the range includes 1, we cannot tell.

2 · A significance test

Gives a p-value: how surprising the difference would be if the two forecasts were equally good.

3 · Many models, many chances

We compare about 15 models. Test enough and some look good by luck, so we make the test stricter. After that, none beats persistence.

4 · "Not different" ≠ "the same"

To say two models are the same, their range must stay within about 10% of each other.

Example from the paper

Residual MLP: ratio 0.96, range [0.93, 1.00]. It only just reaches 1.

Say

A lower number is not automatically better. We ask how much it would change with different months, we correct for comparing many models, and to say two models are the same we require them to stay within ten percent. The technical names are bootstrap, Diebold-Mariano test, adjustment correction and equivalence testing, all in the paper.

Checking survival forecasts

AUROC: can the model rank who survives?

AUROC = the chance that a random survivor gets a higher survival score than a random dissolver.

  • 0.5 = coin flip
  • 1.0 = perfect ranking
Illustrative

Survivors score 0.7 and 0.4; dissolved score 0.5 and 0.2. Of 4 pairs, 3 are ranked correctly → AUROC = 0.75.

In our data: hazard 0.65, logistic 0.66, GBDT 0.67, neural survival heads 0.55–0.62, "last month's survival rate" 0.53. Survival is only weakly predictable. The paper also reports Brier score and calibration.

Say

AUROC checks ranking. A coin flip is 0.5. Our best simple models reach about 0.65, which is modest, and the neural survival heads are weaker.

Interpretation

Why did sophisticated models not win?

1 · Graph information

GCN, SAGE and GBDT-with-neighbours all failed to improve. Dense meta-graph → averaging may blur the clique's own tension, but SAGE (which avoids that) did not help either.

2 · Memory (GRU)

Short histories (1–2 predecessors); equivalent to no GRU.

3 · Survival head

Two-stage ≈ cond-only: the classifier does not change the survivors' tension forecast.

4 · Persistence is strong

Tension is sticky (r ≈ 0.67–0.72), persistence has zero parameters and tracks any level shift.

5 · Residual start matters

Without it (plain GCN) models are 15% worse; with it they converge to persistence.

6 · Small data regime

689 cliques: a model with many weights competes with a zero-parameter baseline. A larger dataset might change the answer (hypothesis).

Say

Six readings. Items one to three are directly tested; four to six are interpretations. The paper says persistence is hard to beat and warns that a bigger dataset might differ.

The deployable result

Why survival must be forecast

Forecasting only survivors is not the real problem: in practice you must forecast every clique, including those that will vanish.

Persistence alone predicts tension for groups that will not exist → large error (it is not a fair reference for Protocol A).

Fair reference: persistence × (training) base survival rate: MSE 19.42.

ŷiA = p̂i · T(k)(Ci)
paper

Replace the base rate with a clique-specific survival probability p̂i. The expectation logic: E[outcome] = P(survive) × T + P(dissolve) × 0.

Which survival forecasts?

AUROC: logistic 0.66, hazard 0.65, GBDT 0.67; neural heads 0.55–0.62; last month's rate 0.53 (≈ coin flip). Survival is weakly predictable, mainly from age, size and tension.

Say

In real use you cannot choose only survivors. So Protocol A scores everything. The best simple reference is persistence times a survival probability. Which probability matters, and a hazard model with clique age does best among simple ones.

Robustness

Other group definitions and a normalized target

Same comparison for five group definitions × raw / normalized target (T̃ = T / max(K−1, 1)).

Raw target

Residual MLP and GCN ratios stay within 0.92–1.09 in every definition; none significantly better than persistence after adjustment. Wide intervals for percolation/Louvain (51 and 69 survivors).

Normalized target

Neural models are worse than persistence (MLP ratios 1.12–1.84; one exception: GCN on cliques not in one email, 0.99). Recalibrated persistence and ridge AR do 10–18% better for clique-based definitions (none significant after adjustment).

Why linear models win there (plausible)

Average coupling tends to drift back toward typical values month to month, which a straight line captures; residual networks stay near persistence because early stopping ends before they learn it. (Interpretation, not tested.)

The only setting where any forecast beat persistence by more than a few percent was a two-parameter linear model.

Say

On the raw target the conclusion holds for all five definitions. On the normalized target the neural models do worse, and only the straight-line models help, with no correction-surviving evidence. The explanation is plausible, not proven.

Honest boundaries

Demonstrated vs. hypothesis

Demonstrated (in this data)

  • ~60% of cliques have no next-month match; part is measurement.
  • Protocol B and short windows can mislead (48% "better" vs 15% worse).
  • Tested neural configurations did not reliably beat persistence; graph, memory and survival head added nothing measurable.
  • A hazard-weighted persistence is the best deployable forecast tried (suggestive).

Hypotheses / open

  • Over-smoothing as the cause (partial at best).
  • Larger data or other architectures might beat persistence.
  • ICCS/SRI as silo measures: untested.
  • Generalisation to other companies or horizons: untested.
  • Why linear models help on normalized tension: interpretation.

Other limits: custodian sample; two hyperparameters tuned on one split; no published temporal GNN (EvolveGCN, DySAT, ROLAND) compared; pairwise projection of emails.

Conclusion

Main findings

  1. Groups turn over fast (about 60% in a month), partly because of how groups are defined (55% are single emails), so the target is often undefined.
  2. Evaluation can mislead: Protocol B and a short collapse window made a plain GCN look 48% better; over 24 rolling origins it is 15% worse.
  3. Properly trained neural models converged to persistence: residual MLP, GCN, SAGE and GCN+GRU are within ~±4% and equivalent.
  4. Graph, memory and the survival head added nothing measurable.
  5. Best deployable forecast: persistence × hazard survival probability, ≈ 17% lower MSE (suggestive; adjusted p = 0.54).

Say

Five findings. Emphasise the first two as the methodological message and the last as the practical one.

Conclusion

Contribution, failures and the practical model

What it contributes

A clean way to evaluate forecasts when units disappear (Protocols A/B, rolling origin, equivalence), fair controls, and a measurement analysis of turnover.

What did not work

Graph neural networks, GraphSAGE, GRU memory and the hurdle survival head did not reliably beat persistence on this dataset.

Most useful practical model

Persistence × a simple survival probability (hazard model on clique age, size, tension, group count). Two lines of code, no deep learning.

For researchers

Report persistence first; score dissolved units; test with equivalence margins; validate scores externally.

Say

The positive contribution is methodological and a very simple practical forecast; the negative result is that the sophisticated models did not reliably help here.

Take-home message

Groups vanish so quickly that tension forecasting is mostly about guessing who survives. On this data, properly trained graph and temporal networks add nothing over copying this month's value.

The best forecast we found is persistence scaled by a simple survival probability.

Discussion

Questions? — likely ones and short answers

Why not a bigger GNN? Properly trained, tuned, residual start; best validation was epoch 0 for GCN/SAGE. Small data (689 cliques); larger data might differ.

Is Protocol B wrong? No; it conditions on the future, so it only isolates the tension part.

Is the 17% significant? Bootstrap interval excludes 1; adjusted p = 0.54 → suggestive.

Did you prove over-smoothing? No: plausible, partial; SAGE did not help either.

Do ICCS/SRI measure silos? Not shown; they measure coupling; need external labels.

Does this generalise? Unknown: one company, one period; second dataset is future work.
